MAS Pace Chart: Converting Speeds (km/h & mph) to 400m Lap Splits & Mile Paces

⏱️ Reading Time: 6 min 📐 The 1440 Rule: $1440 / \text{Speed} = \text{Lap Time (s)}$ 🏃 Standards: 10.0 to 22.0 km/h 📅 Updated: Oct 2026

On a 400-meter track, runners and coaches cannot read speedometers — they read stopwatch lap split times.

The universal mathematical formula to calculate 400m lap time from speed is: 400m Lap Time (seconds) = 1440 / Speed (km/h). For example, running at 15.0 km/h requires crossing the 400m line in exactly 96.0 seconds (1:36), with 200m at 48.0s and 100m at 24.0s. Whether you are coaching 30 high school students during an athletics unit, prepping club runners for a sub-20 minute 5K, or pacing intermittent interval blocks for soccer athletes, having a precise grasp of lap split pacing is the difference between scientific training and chaotic guessing. Below is the complete mathematical framework, master conversion tables, and track geometry rules for mastering track splits.

1. The Math of Track Pacing: The "1440" Rule

Why is 1440 the magic constant in track athletics? The derivation is simple:

📐 Deriving the 400m Lap Split Formula

One hour contains $3,600\text{ seconds}$. A standard track lap in Lane 1 is exactly $400\text{ meters}$ (or $0.4\text{ kilometers}$).

$$\text{Time for 400m (seconds)} = \frac{0.4\text{ km} \times 3,600\text{ s}}{\text{Speed (km/h)}} = \frac{1,440}{\text{Speed (km/h)}}$$

Sub-splits are derived by division:

  • 200m Split (seconds): $720 / \text{Speed (km/h)}$
  • 100m Split (seconds): $360 / \text{Speed (km/h)}$
  • 1,000m Split (seconds): $3,600 / \text{Speed (km/h)}$

For imperial runners calculating in miles per hour (mph), one mile is $1,609.344\text{ meters}$. A 400m lap represents approximately 0.2485 miles: $\text{Time for 400m (seconds)} \approx 894.8 / \text{Speed (mph)}$. 3. Calculating Paces by % of MAS (Intensity Adjustments)

In workout planning, you almost never prescribe 100% MAS for long repetitions. Instead, you scale your splits according to the physiological target:

🟢

80% – 85% MAS (Tempo & Threshold)

Used for continuous tempo runs and long cruising intervals ($3 \times 2,000\text{m}$). Formula: $\text{Target Speed} = \text{MAS} \times 0.85$. E.g. for a 16 km/h MAS runner, tempo speed is 13.6 km/h (106s per 400m lap).

🟡

90% – 95% MAS (VO₂max Capacity)

Used for 800m to 1,200m repetitions ($5 \times 1,000\text{m}$). Target Speed = $\text{MAS} \times 0.95$. For 16 km/h MAS: 15.2 km/h (94.7s per 400m lap / 3:57 per 1,000m).

🔴

100% MAS (True Aerobic Power)

Used for classic short intervals (30s/30s or 400m reps). Target Speed = $16.0\text{ km/h}$ (90s per 400m lap / 22.5s per 100m).

⚡

105% – 115% MAS (Supramaximal HIIT)

Used for short intermittent sprints (15s/15s or 10s/20s). For 110% of 16 km/h MAS: speed is 17.6 km/h (81.8s 400m pace / 20.5s per 100m).

4. Track Geometry Warning: The Outside Lane Trap

One of the most frequent coaching mistakes is running athletes in outside lanes without recalculating split distances:

📐 Lane Radius & Distance Mathematics

According to World Athletics rules, a 400m track is measured 30 centimeters out from the inner rail in Lane 1 only. Each consecutive lane features a wider curve radius, adding distance on every single turn:

  • Lane 1: 400.00 meters per lap
  • Lane 2: 407.04 meters ($+7.04\text{m}$ per lap)
  • Lane 3: 414.70 meters ($+14.70\text{m}$ per lap)
  • Lane 4: 422.37 meters ($+22.37\text{m}$ per lap)
  • Lane 5: 430.03 meters ($+30.03\text{m}$ per lap)
  • Lane 8: 453.03 meters ($+53.03\text{m}$ per lap — over 13% farther!)

The Coaching Implication: If a coach tells a runner in Lane 4 to run a 400m lap in 90 seconds, but the runner circles Lane 4, they actually covered 422 meters. Their true speed was 16.9 km/h instead of 16.0 km/h, causing premature exhaustion! Always place staggered cone markers or restrict time trials to Lane 1.

5. How to Coach Pacing on Track: Auditory & Visual Cueing

1

The 100m Cone Marking System

Place cones at the start line, the 100m mark (exit of turn 1), the 200m mark (middle of the back straight), and the 300m mark (entry of home straight). Divide the 400m lap time by 4.

2

The Whistle / Horn Synchronization

For group classes, blow a whistle at the precise 100m split time (e.g., every 24 seconds for a 96-second lap group). If a runner is 5 meters past the cone when the whistle blows, they are running too fast; if they have not reached it, they must accelerate.

3

Developing "Internal Kinesthesia"

After 3 to 4 paced workouts, challenge runners to run a 400m lap at 90% MAS without looking at their watch. Skilled runners develop the ability to hit target lap splits within $\pm 1\text{ second}$ through breathing rhythm and stride cadence.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Why is it better to count 100m splits rather than 400m lap times?

A runner can run the first 200m 6 seconds too fast and the second 200m 6 seconds too slow, crossing the 400m line in the exact planned time but experiencing severe lactic fatigue. 100m checkpoints enforce true even-pacing and prevent the common "sprint and die" mistake.

How do I adjust lap times for a 200m or 250m school track?

For a 200m track: $\text{Lap Time (s)} = 720 / \text{Speed (km/h)}$. For a 250m track: $\text{Lap Time (s)} = 900 / \text{Speed (km/h)}$. For example, at 15.0 km/h on a 250m track, each lap takes exactly 60.0 seconds.

How does wind affect 400m track lap times?

Because track ovals are circular, runners face both a headwind on one straight and a tailwind on the other. However, the aerodynamic drag penalty of a headwind is greater than the assistance of a tailwind. A 15 km/h wind typically adds 1.5 to 3.0 seconds per 400m lap.

Can I use GPS watches instead of track lap splits?

GPS watches struggle with instant pace on circular 400m ovals due to satellite interpolation smoothing. A watch will often display a pace that lags 10 to 15 seconds behind real-time speed. Using a simple stopwatch lap counter paired with physical track markers is substantially more accurate.
