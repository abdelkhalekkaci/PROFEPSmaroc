Youth MAS Testing in Physical Education: Age Norms & Lesson Plans

By Prof EPS Sports Science Editorial • October 2, 2026 • ⏱️ 13 min read • 🏫 Secondary School Norms (11-18)

⚡ The PE Teacher's Challenge & Pedagogical Solution

Teaching endurance to adolescent classes of 30+ students presents unique challenges: wide disparities in physical maturity, varying motivation, and risk of student disengagement. By grounding school endurance units in Maximal Aerobic Speed (MAS / VMA), teachers can move away from punitive continuous track laps and implement differentiated, equal-time intermittent running. Knowing adolescent norms (ages 11 to 18) and accounting for Peak Height Velocity (PHV) ensures fair, motivating, and curriculum-compliant assessment.

1. The Maturation Factor: Peak Height Velocity (PHV) & Aerobic Fitness

One of the most frequent mistakes in school athletics is evaluating students strictly by chronological age rather than biological maturation. Between ages 11 and 16, a single physical education class can contain students who are biologically 2 years delayed alongside peers who are 2 years advanced.

⚠️ Understanding Peak Height Velocity (PHV)

Peak Height Velocity (PHV) is the period of most rapid somatic growth during the adolescent spurt:

  • Girls: Typically reach PHV between ages 11.5 and 13.0 years. Relative $\text{VO}_2\text{max}$ (in $\text{ml/kg/min}$) often plateaus or slightly declines as post-pubertal fat mass accumulates.
  • Boys: Typically reach PHV between ages 13.5 and 15.0 years. Testosterone surges trigger significant skeletal muscle hypertrophy, myocardial expansion, and increased hemoglobin concentrations, leading to a marked increase in MAS.

During the peak PHV phase ("the adolescent awkwardness"), students experience temporary disruptions in stride length, cadence, and running economy ($C_r$). Teachers must avoid penalizing students undergoing active growth spurts.

3. Pedagogical Class Organization: The "Buddy / Co-Observer" System

Testing 30+ students at once on a standard 200m or 400m track without assistant coaches or electronic timing gates requires a disciplined pedagogical setup:

1

Pair into "Coureurs" and "Témoins"

Divide the class into pairs. Student A runs while Student B serves as the active recorder, counting laps, noting split times, and providing vocal encouragement.

2

Install 25m or 50m Numbered Markers

Place bright numbered cones every 25 meters around the track. When the 6-minute whistle blows, runners immediately freeze at their last passed marker.

3

Apply the Distance/100 Rule

If Student A completes 3 full 400m laps plus passes the 150m cone ($1,350\text{ meters}$ total), their MAS is instantly computed as: $1350 \div 100 = \mathbf{13.5\text{ km/h}}$.

4

Swap Roles with Zero Downtime

After a 5-minute recovery, Student B runs while Student A records. The entire class is tested with scientific accuracy within a single 45-minute lesson.

4. Moving Away from Continuous Jogging: Differentiated Intermittent Grids

Traditional physical education units that mandate "run for 20 minutes without stopping" produce negative educational outcomes: fit students jog bored below their threshold, while overweight or deconditioned students drop to a walk within 4 minutes, experiencing public embarrassment.

🎯 The Equal-Time Differentiated Grid Method

Instead of continuous running, organize the track into 4 speed corridors for intermittent intervals (e.g. 15s work : 15s rest or 30s work : 30s rest):

  • Corridor A (MAS 15-16 km/h): 15s distance = 65 meters
  • Corridor B (MAS 13-14 km/h): 15s distance = 56 meters
  • Corridor C (MAS 11-12 km/h): 15s distance = 48 meters
  • Corridor D (MAS 9-10 km/h): 15s distance = 40 meters

The pedagogical magic: The teacher blows one single whistle. Every student starts together, runs for 15 seconds, arrives at their specific cone, rests for 15 seconds, and returns together. No student finishes last, no student is left behind, and all students work at their optimal physiological target.

Modern physical education curricula (such as official French and Moroccan ministerial directives, Baccalauréat EPS, and GCSE Physical Education) do not assess students solely on raw athletic performance. True competency is demonstrated when a student learns to know their body, predict their pace, and run with precision:
