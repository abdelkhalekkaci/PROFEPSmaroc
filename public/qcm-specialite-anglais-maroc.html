B) Alliteration, Assonance, Consonance, Rhyme, and Meter
C) Prefixation, Suffixation, Infixation, Circumfixation, and Ablaut
D) Intonation, Pitch, Loudness, Tempo, and Rhythm
💡 Justification didactique : Note the distinction between 'Anaphoric reference' (pointing backward to a preceding antecedent in the text) and 'Cataphoric reference' (pointing forward to a later element).
Q76 Sociolinguistics · Diglossia vs Bilingualism Sociolinguistics (Ferguson, 1959)

In sociolinguistics, what defines a 'Diglossic' situation (as conceptualized by Charles Ferguson)?

A) The stable functional specialization of two varieties of a language (or two languages) within a speech community: a High (H) variety used in formal/official domains and a Low (L) variety used in everyday informal conversation ✓ Réponse officielle
B) An individual's inability to speak any language fluently
C) A geographical border line separating two regional pronunciations of a vowel
D) The complete extinction of a minority language
💡 Justification didactique : Ferguson illustrated classic diglossia with Arabic (Standard Fuṣḥā as H vs regional Darija/vernaculars as L), Swiss German, Haitian Creole, and Modern Greek.
Q77 Sociolinguistics · Code-Switching vs Borrowing Language Contact & Bilingualism

How do sociolinguists distinguish 'Code-switching' from 'Lexical Borrowing'?

A) Code-switching is the alternation between two or more languages/varieties within a single conversation or sentence by bilingual speakers, whereas a borrowed word (loanword) becomes phonologically and morphologically integrated into the recipient language and is used even by monolinguals ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Code-switching indicates mental confusion and lack of competence in both languages
C) Borrowing only happens in written poetry
D) There is no difference between code-switching and pidginization
💡 Justification didactique : Research (Myers-Scotton, Poplack) demonstrates that intrasentential and intersentential code-switching is rule-governed and fulfills rich social and discourse functions.
Q78 Sociolinguistics · Pidgins vs Creoles Contact Languages

What is the linguistic transition that turns a 'Pidgin' into a 'Creole'?

A) Nativization (creolization): when a contact pidgin (which initially had no native speakers and a simplified grammar) becomes the first/native language of a new generation of children and expands structurally and lexically ✓ Réponse officielle
B) When a standard national language loses all its speakers and dies out
C) When a regional dialect is adopted by the royal court
D) When a language is written down in the Latin alphabet for the first time
💡 Justification didactique : Unlike pidgins (restricted contact codes for trade/labor), creoles possess full native-speaker complexity and serve all communicative domains.
Q79 Psycholinguistics · Broca's Aphasia vs Wernicke's Aphasia Neurolinguistics

How do the clinical linguistic profiles of 'Broca's Aphasia' and 'Wernicke's Aphasia' contrast?

A) Broca's aphasia (anterior frontal lesion) causes non-fluent, effortful, telegraphic speech (agrammatism) with relatively preserved comprehension, whereas Wernicke's aphasia (posterior temporal lesion) produces fluent but semantically empty/paraphasic speech with severe comprehension deficit ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Broca's aphasia causes total deafness, whereas Wernicke's aphasia causes paralysis of the legs
C) Both conditions improve grammatical complexity and vocabulary size
D) Wernicke's aphasia prevents any vocal sound from being produced
💡 Justification didactique : This double dissociation between anterior production/syntactic planning (Broca's area) and posterior auditory comprehension/lexical selection (Wernicke's area) is foundational in neurolinguistics.
Q80 First Language Acquisition · Overgeneralization (U-Shaped Development) Psycholinguistics & Child Language

When a young child who previously said 'went' and 'feet' starts saying 'goed' and 'foots' before later returning to 'went' and 'feet', what does this 'U-shaped curve' demonstrate?

A) That the child is no longer merely imitating memorized forms, but has internalized a productive morphological rule (regular past '-ed' / plural '-s') and overgeneralized it before learning exceptions ✓ Réponse officielle
B) That the child is suffering from progressive memory loss
C) That language acquisition is 100% passive mimicry of adult errors, since adults say 'goed'
D) That children should be forbidden from speaking until age seven
💡 Justification didactique : Creative overgeneralizations ('mans', 'holded') were key evidence in Chomsky's critique of Skinner's behaviorism, showing that children actively construct an internal grammar.
Q81 Historical Linguistics · The Great Vowel Shift History of the English Language

What major phonological change occurred between Middle English (Chaucer's era, c. 1400) and Modern English, explaining why English vowel spelling often differs from continental European values?

A) The Great Vowel Shift: a systematic chain shift in which all seven Middle English long vowels were raised in tongue height or diphthongized (e.g., /iː/ → /aɪ/ in 'time', /uː/ → /aʊ/ in 'house') ✓ Réponse officielle
B) The sudden disappearance of all consonant clusters from spoken English
C) The replacement of Germanic syntax by Arabic word order in the 15th century
D) The invention of the printing press by William Shakespeare in 1750
💡 Justification didactique : Because William Caxton introduced printing to England (1476) while the Great Vowel Shift was underway, English orthography froze largely Middle English vowel spellings while pronunciation shifted.
Q82 Semantics · Thematic (Theta) Roles Syntax-Semantics Interface

In the passive sentence 'The window was broken by the storm with a fallen branch', what thematic roles are assigned to 'the window', 'the storm', and 'a fallen branch'?

A) 'The window' = Patient/Theme (undergoing the change of state); 'the storm' = Force/Causer; 'a fallen branch' = Instrument ✓ Réponse officielle
B) 'The window' = Agent (performing an intentional action) because it is the grammatical subject
C) 'The storm' = Experiencer; 'a fallen branch' = Goal
D) All three noun phrases carry the Beneficiary role
💡 Justification didactique : Thematic roles remain invariant under passivization: although 'the window' moves to grammatical Subject position, it retains its semantic Patient/Theme role.
Q83 Grammar · Ergative (Unaccusative) Verbs Lexical-Syntactic Alternations

Which class of English verbs allows the object of the transitive structure to become the subject of the intransitive structure without passive morphology (e.g., 'The sun melted the ice' ↔ 'The ice melted')?

A) Ergative (or causative-inchoative alternating) verbs such as 'melt, break, open, boil, sink' ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Purely unergative verbs such as 'laugh, sneeze, cough, sleep'
C) Modal auxiliary verbs such as 'must, may, shall'
D) Ditransitive verbs that never allow an intransitive use
💡 Justification didactique : In the intransitive alternant ('The door opened', 'The ship sank'), the grammatical subject is semantically the Patient/Theme undergoing the change of state.
Q84 Grammar · Cleft vs Pseudo-Cleft (Wh-Cleft) Sentences Information Packaging in English

Transform the neutral sentence 'Sofia bought a new laptop yesterday' into both an 'It-cleft' (focusing on 'a new laptop') and a 'Pseudo-cleft (Wh-cleft)':

A) It-cleft: 'It was a new laptop that Sofia bought yesterday' | Pseudo-cleft: 'What Sofia bought yesterday was a new laptop' ✓ Réponse officielle
B) It-cleft: 'Sofia did buy a laptop' | Pseudo-cleft: 'A laptop was bought by Sofia'
C) It-cleft: 'There was a laptop bought' | Pseudo-cleft: 'Had Sofia bought a laptop'
D) Both transformations are ungrammatical in standard English
💡 Justification didactique : Clefting and pseudo-clefting are syntactic information-packaging devices used to highlight a specific constituent as new or contrastive focus.
Q85 Grammar · Participial Clauses & Dangling Modifiers Advanced Syntax & Style

Why is the sentence 'Walking down the street, the tall skyscrapers amazed the tourists' considered to contain a 'dangling participle' (unattached modifier)?

A) Because the implicit subject of the initial participial clause 'Walking down the street' must corefer with the grammatical subject of the main clause, making it absurdly imply that 'the tall skyscrapers' were walking ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Because English sentences can never begin with an '-ing' verb form
C) Because the verb 'amazed' requires the preposition 'at' in the active voice
D) Because 'tourists' is an uncountable noun
💡 Justification didactique : To repair the dangling modifier, the performer of the participial action must be made the subject of the matrix clause: 'Walking down the street, the tourists were amazed by the tall skyscrapers.'
Q86 Grammar · Gerund vs Infinitive Meaning Contrast Verb Complementation

What is the clear difference in meaning between 'He stopped smoking' and 'He stopped to smoke'?

A) 'He stopped smoking' (gerund object) means he quit the habit of smoking; 'He stopped to smoke' (infinitive of purpose) means he halted another activity in order to smoke ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Both sentences have the exact same meaning in British and American English
C) 'He stopped smoking' refers to the future, whereas 'He stopped to smoke' refers to the past
D) 'To smoke' is a preposition in both sentences
💡 Justification didactique : Similarly, 'remember/forget/regret + gerund' looks back at a prior event ('I remember locking the door'), whereas '+ to-infinitive' looks forward to a duty ('Remember to lock the door').
Q87 Grammar · Countable vs Uncountable (Mass) Nouns English Nominal System

Which of the following sets consists entirely of nouns that are strictly uncountable (mass nouns) in standard English and cannot take the indefinite article *'a/an' or a plural *'-s'?

A) 'Information, advice, furniture, luggage, equipment, research, evidence' ✓ Réponse officielle
B) 'Suggestion, idea, chair, suitcase, tool, study, fact'
C) 'Book, table, computer, student, teacher, classroom'
D) 'Hour, minute, second, day, week, month'
💡 Justification didactique : Because 'advice, information, furniture, equipment, luggage' are mass nouns in English (unlike their countable French/Arabic equivalents), individuation requires partitives ('a piece of advice/information').
Q88 Literature & Stylistics · Figures of Speech (Metonymy vs Synecdoche) Rhetoric & Literary Stylistics

What distinguishes 'Metonymy' (e.g., 'The White House issued a statement', 'The pen is mightier than the sword') from 'Metaphor'?

A) Metonymy substitutes an associated or contiguous attribute/institution for the thing meant, whereas metaphor transfers qualities across two unrelated conceptual domains based on similarity ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Metonymy always uses the words 'like' or 'as', whereas simile never does
C) Metonymy is a phonetic repetition of vowel sounds
D) Metaphor is restricted to scientific textbooks
💡 Justification didactique : Roman Jakobson contrasted the metaphoric pole (selection/similarity) with the metonymic pole (combination/contiguity; with synecdoche as part-for-whole or whole-for-part).
Q89 Literary Stylistics · Oxymoron vs Paradox Figures of Speech

Which expression is a classic example of an 'Oxymoron'?

A) 'Deafening silence' or 'bittersweet' (the compressed syntactic juxtaposition of two contradictory terms in a single phrase) ✓ Réponse officielle
B) 'She is as brave as a lion' (explicit comparison using 'as')
C) 'The wind whispered through the trees' (personification)
D) 'Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers' (alliteration)
💡 Justification didactique : While a paradox is a broader self-contradictory statement that reveals a deeper truth ('Less is more'), an oxymoron tightly yokes two opposite words together ('living dead', 'open secret').
Q90 Poetics · Iambic Pentameter English Prosody & Shakespearean Verse

How is a regular line of 'Iambic Pentameter' (such as Shakespeare's 'Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?') metrically structured?

A) Five metrical feet (ten syllables total), where each foot is an iamb consisting of an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable (da-DUM × 5) ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Ten stressed syllables followed by ten unstressed syllables
C) Three trochaic feet per line with no rhythm
D) Free verse without any regular stress pattern
💡 Justification didactique : Unrhymed iambic pentameter is known as 'Blank Verse' (the staple of Shakespearean drama and Milton's 'Paradise Lost'), while fourteen lines ending in a rhyming couplet form the Shakespearean sonnet.
Q91 Narrative Theory · Stream of Consciousness & Free Indirect Discourse Modernist Literary Technique

In narrative stylistics (e.g., Jane Austen, Virginia Woolf, James Joyce), what characterizes 'Free Indirect Discourse' (style indirect libre)?

A) Third-person narration and past tense blend seamlessly with the character's own inner voice, idioms, exclamations, and deictics without reporting clauses like 'she thought that' ✓ Réponse officielle
B) The narrator speaks exclusively in the first-person plural 'we'
C) All characters' thoughts are enclosed in quotation marks with 'he said'
D) The novel is written entirely in stage directions
💡 Justification didactique : Free Indirect Discourse creates dual-voice ambiguity and empathy by merging the narrator's grammatical frame with the character's subjective consciousness.
Q92 Structuralism · Saussure's Dichotomies General Linguistics (F. de Saussure, 1916)

In Ferdinand de Saussure's 'Course in General Linguistics', what is the distinction between 'Synchronic' and 'Diachronic' linguistics?

A) Synchronic linguistics studies a language system as a structured whole at a specific point in time, whereas diachronic linguistics studies how a language evolves historically across time ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Synchronic linguistics studies only written texts, whereas diachronic linguistics studies only spoken dialects
C) Synchronic refers to individual speech ('parole'), whereas diachronic refers to the abstract system ('langue')
D) Saussure argued that linguistic signs have a natural, non-arbitrary link between signifier and signified
💡 Justification didactique : Saussure established key structuralist oppositions: Langue vs Parole, Signifier (acoustic image) vs Signified (concept), Syntagmatic vs Paradigmatic (associative) relations, and Synchronic vs Diachronic analysis.
Q93 Chomsky · Competence vs Performance Generative Linguistics (Chomsky, 1965)

How does Noam Chomsky distinguish linguistic 'Competence' (later I-language) from 'Performance' (E-language)?

A) Competence is the speaker-hearer's tacit mental knowledge of grammatical rules and principles, whereas performance is the actual use of language in concrete situations, subject to memory limits, slips, and distractions ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Competence means speaking rapidly on stage, whereas performance means passing a written spelling test
C) Performance is always error-free, whereas competence consists of hesitations and false starts
D) Chomsky argued that human language is learned entirely through operant conditioning without innate faculty
💡 Justification didactique : Chomsky's distinction parallels Saussure's Langue/Parole but reframes competence cognitively as a generative mental grammar capable of producing an infinite number of novel sentences from finite means.
Q94 Design Features of Human Language · Charles Hockett General Linguistics

Among Charles Hockett's design features of human language, what do 'Displacement' and 'Duality of Patterning (Double Articulation)' refer to?

A) Displacement is the ability to refer to things remote in time and space (past, future, hypothetical); Duality of Patterning is the combination of meaningless phonemes into meaningful morphemes and words ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Displacement means moving from one country to another; Duality means speaking two languages
C) Honeybees' waggle dance possesses full duality of patterning and syntactic recursion
D) Duality of patterning means every English word has two opposite meanings
💡 Justification didactique : André Martinet termed this 'Double Articulation': a small inventory of ~44 distinctive, meaningless phonemes combines to form tens of thousands of meaningful lexical items.
Q95 Functional Grammar · Halliday's Three Metafunctions Systemic Functional Linguistics (M.A.K. Halliday)

In Halliday's Systemic Functional Grammar, what are the three simultaneous 'Metafunctions' fulfilled by language?

A) Ideational (representing experience of the world), Interpersonal (enacting social relationships and attitudes), and Textual (organizing the message into coherent discourse via Theme/Rheme) ✓ Réponse officielle
B) Phonological, Morphological, and Orthographic metafunctions
C) Past, Present, and Future metafunctions
D) Literal, Metaphorical, and Ironical metafunctions
💡 Justification didactique : Halliday analyzes every clause across three strands: Transitivity (Ideational: Actor, Process, Goal), Mood/Modality (Interpersonal), and Theme-Rheme structure (Textual).
Q96 Corpus Linguistics · Concordancing & Collocation Strength Applied Linguistics

In corpus linguistics, what is a 'Concordance (KWIC: Key Word in Context)' used for?

A) To display numerous authentic corpus lines centered on a node word, revealing its authentic collocations, colligations, semantic prosody, and frequency patterns ✓ Réponse officielle

C) To translate literary poetry automatically without human review
D) To measure the acoustic decibel level of a speaker's voice
💡 Justification didactique : KWIC concordances empirically uncover 'semantic prosody' (e.g., how the verb 'cause' overwhelmingly collocates with negative outcomes like 'accident, damage, crisis').
Q97 Contrastive Analysis vs Error Analysis · Corder & Selinker Applied Linguistics & SLA

Why did Pit Corder's 'Error Analysis' and Larry Selinker's 'Interlanguage' theory supersede the strong version of Lado's 'Contrastive Analysis Hypothesis'?

A) Because empirical studies showed that many L2 errors are not caused by L1 transfer, but are developmental intralingual errors reflecting the learner's evolving, systematic 'interlanguage' ✓ Réponse officielle

D) Because Error Analysis treats mistakes and systematic errors as identical
💡 Justification didactique : Pit Corder also drew the classic distinction between a 'Mistake' (a random performance slip that the learner can self-correct) and an 'Error' (a systematic gap in underlying competence).
Q98 Fossilization in Interlanguage · Larry Selinker (1972) Second Language Acquisition

What did Larry Selinker mean by 'Fossilization' in adult second language acquisition?

D) The forgetting of one's native language in childhood
💡 Justification didactique : Fossilization often occurs when a learner's interlanguage form is communicatively sufficient to get meaning across, reducing the internal pressure to restructure toward the target norm.

In Braj Kachru's model of 'World Englishes' (Inner Circle, Outer Circle, Expanding Circle), into which circle does Morocco fall, and why?

Q100 Pragmatics · Deixis (Person, Spatial, Temporal, Discourse, Social) Pragmatics

Why are expressions such as 'I, you, here, there, now, yesterday, this chapter' classified as 'Deictic expressions' (indexicals)?
